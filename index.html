Landing a rocket with convex optimization

The guidance problem behind a Falcon 9 landing: burn the least fuel, touch the pad exactly, and never let the engine fall below its minimum throttle. It starts out non-convex — and a 2007 result called lossless convexification turns it into a problem a solver cracks to the global optimum in about 100 milliseconds. 01 · The problem

Fly a rocket to the pad on the least fuel

A booster is falling toward a landing site. It can point and throttle its engine, but every second of thrust burns propellant it can't get back. The guidance computer has to choose the thrust profile that brings the vehicle to rest, exactly on the pad, while burning as little fuel as possible — inside a fixed flight time, and respecting what the hardware can actually do: a throttle floor and ceiling, a limited gimbal angle, a glideslope it can't dip below.

Written honestly, the state is position, velocity and mass — mass shrinks as you burn — and the cost is the integral of thrust. Minimize fuel, subject to the dynamics and the hardware. Simple to state. The trouble is one word: non-convex.

02 · Why it's hard

A real engine can't switch off — and that punches a hole

A rocket engine has a minimum stable thrust: below it, combustion is unstable and the engine flames out. So the allowed thrust vectors satisfy ρmin ≤ ‖T‖ ≤ ρmax — everything between two spheres. That set is an annulus, a ring with a hole in the middle. And a ring is not convex.

Two opposite thrusts on the inner ring average to the midpoint in the forbidden hole (engine off) — the chord leaves the set, the signature of non-convexity. Now watch the fix in 3-D: lift the flat ring into the (T, σ) cone and the midpoint rides up inside. The cone ‖T‖ ≤ σ is convex, so the chord can't escape any more — same physics, a convex set. That lift is lossless convexification, next.
03 · The fix

Lossless convexification — lift the ring into a cone

The trick (Açıkmeşe & Ploen, JPL 2007): introduce a slack variable σ, demand ‖T‖ ≤ σ — a second-order cone, which is convex — and bound ρmin ≤ σ ≤ ρmax instead. Wherever the norm ‖T‖ appeared, use σ. Geometrically, you've lifted the flat ring into a 3-D cone, and the hole closes: the chord that used to escape now stays inside.

minT,σ ∫0tf σdt s.t. ‖T‖≤σ (cone, convex) s.t. ρmin≤ σ≤ ρmax

The magic word is lossless: it's provable that at the optimum σ* = ‖T*‖ exactly — the relaxation is tight, you lose nothing. A second change of variables (z = ln m, working in log-mass) removes the last nonlinearity, the thrust-over-mass term. What's left is a plain second-order cone program — the kind convex solvers dispatch in milliseconds with a global-optimum certificate.

04 · The result

The optimal burn is bang-bang

Solve it and the optimal throttle isn't smooth — it's bang-bang: full thrust to kill the incoming velocity, coast at minimum through the middle, then full thrust again for the touchdown burn. 05 · Robustness

What if the approach isn't nominal?

Real descents are dispersed. I ran a Monte-Carlo of 190 approaches with the initial position and velocity kicked by Gaussians (σr = 100 m, σv = 10 m/s per axis), flight time fixed at 75 s. Each case is solved twice: first for an exact landing; if that's infeasible, a Minimum-Landing-Error fallback that relaxes "hit the pad" into a soft penalty and just tries to touch down in one piece, off-target.

Green nails the pad; orange are MLE rescues that land safely but off-target; the rest can't brake to the pad inside a fixed 75 s and would need a longer burn. The fallback converts about a third of the would-be failures into survivable landings — the difference between a lost vehicle and a scuff on the paint. A subtlety worth flagging: the surviving fuel average sits below nominal, pure survival bias — the fastest, hardest cases are exactly the ones that drop out.
